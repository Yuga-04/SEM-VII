# 28(b) Discuss the budgetary and non-budgetary control techniques and explain their applications in management. (15 Marks)

## 1. Introduction

Control is the process of comparing actual performance with planned performance and taking corrective action when deviations occur.

Budgetary and non-budgetary control techniques help managers monitor activities, control costs, improve efficiency and achieve organizational objectives.

## 2. Budgetary control

Budgetary control is a technique in which actual results are compared with predetermined budgets to identify deviations and take corrective action.

A budget is a financial or quantitative plan prepared for a specific period.

### Types of budgetary control techniques

1\. Revenue and expense budgets

These budgets estimate expected income and operating expenses.

- Revenue budgets estimate sales income.
- Expense budgets estimate costs such as salaries, advertising, electricity and transportation.

Example: A company prepares a monthly sales target of ₹10 lakh and estimates its operating expenses.

2\. Time, space, material and product budgets

These budgets specify the resources required for operations.

- Time budget: Time required to complete work.
- Space budget: Space required for activities.
- Material budget: Materials required for production.
- Product budget: Number of products to be manufactured.

Example: A factory prepares a monthly raw-material budget according to its production target.

3\. Capital expenditure budget

It estimates expenditure on long-term assets such as machinery, buildings and equipment.

Example: A manufacturing company plans its expenditure on a new production machine.

4\. Cash budget

It estimates cash receipts and payments during a specific period.

It helps management ensure that sufficient cash is available to meet expenses.

Example: A business forecasts its cash inflows and outflows for the next three months.

5\. Production budget

It specifies the quantity of products to be manufactured during a given period.

Example: A factory plans to produce 5,000 units in a month based on expected demand.

6\. Flexible budget

A flexible budget adjusts according to the actual level of activity.

It is useful when production or sales volume changes.

Example: A company adjusts its expected material costs when production increases from 1,000 to 1,500 units.

7\. Master budget

A master budget combines different departmental budgets into one overall plan for the organization.

It provides a complete view of expected revenue, expenses, production and financial requirements.

## 3. Advantages of budgetary control

1. Encourages future planning.
2. Improves coordination between departments.
3. Clearly defines responsibilities.
4. Helps compare actual results with planned targets.
5. Identifies deviations and supports corrective action.
6. Improves allocation of limited resources.
7. Supports performance evaluation.
8. Helps reduce unnecessary expenditure.

## 4. Limitations of budgetary control

1. Preparing budgets requires time and effort.
2. Incorrect forecasts may produce unrealistic targets.
3. Employees may feel excessive pressure to achieve targets.
4. Departments may disagree over resource allocation.
5. Budgets may become unsuitable when conditions change.
6. Managers may focus on meeting budget figures rather than improving overall performance.

## 5. Non-budgetary control techniques

Non-budgetary control techniques monitor performance without relying exclusively on budget comparisons.

### 1. Statistical data and reports

Statistical information is used to measure and analyse business performance.

Data can be presented through tables, graphs, charts and reports.

Example: A sales manager uses a graph to compare monthly sales across different regions.

### 2. Break-even analysis

Break-even analysis identifies the level of sales at which total revenue equals total cost.

At the break-even point, the business makes neither a profit nor a loss.

\\[ \text{Break-even units}= \frac{\text{Fixed Costs}}{\text{Selling Price per Unit}-\text{Variable Cost per Unit}} \\]

Example: A company calculates how many units it must sell to cover all production costs.

### 3. Operational audit

An operational audit is a systematic examination of operational activities to evaluate efficiency, effectiveness and compliance with procedures.

Example: An internal audit team examines whether a warehouse follows proper inventory procedures.

### 4. Personal observation

Managers directly observe employees, equipment and work processes to assess performance.

Example: A production manager observes a manufacturing line to identify delays.

### 5. PERT

Program Evaluation and Review Technique (PERT) is used to plan and control complex projects by estimating task durations and identifying the minimum expected project completion time.

Example: A construction company uses PERT to coordinate project activities.

### 6. Gantt chart

A Gantt chart is a bar chart that shows activities against a timeline, including their planned start and finish dates.

Example: A software project manager uses a Gantt chart to monitor development and testing deadlines.

### 7. Performance reports

Performance reports compare actual achievements with established standards and highlight deviations.

Example: A monthly report identifies that production has fallen below the required target.

## 6. Applications in management

Budgetary and non-budgetary techniques are applied to:

- Financial planning and cost control.
- Production and inventory management.
- Sales and marketing performance.
- Employee performance evaluation.
- Project scheduling and monitoring.
- Quality improvement.
- Resource allocation.
- Identification and correction of deviations.

## 7. Conclusion

Budgetary control focuses on planned financial and quantitative targets, while non-budgetary control uses techniques such as statistical reports, break-even analysis, operational audits, PERT and Gantt charts. Combining both methods enables managers to monitor performance, reduce wastage and achieve organizational objectives.

# 29(a) Analyse productivity problems faced by management and explain suitable measures for improving productivity. (15 Marks)

## 1. Introduction

Productivity refers to the relationship between the output produced and the resources used to produce it.

\\[ \text{Productivity}=\frac{\text{Output}}{\text{Input}} \\]

Inputs include labour, materials, machinery, time and money. Productivity improves when an organization produces more output using the same resources or maintains output while reducing resource consumption.

## 2. Productivity problems faced by management

### 1. Lack of employee motivation

Employees who lack motivation may show low interest, poor concentration and reduced effort.

Solution: Provide fair salaries, incentives, recognition and opportunities for promotion.

### 2. Inadequate training and skills

Employees who lack the necessary skills may make mistakes, work slowly and produce poor-quality output.

Solution: Conduct regular training programmes, workshops and practical demonstrations.

### 3. Poor management and planning

Unclear goals, poor scheduling and improper allocation of duties can waste time and resources.

Solution: Set clear targets, prepare suitable work schedules and assign responsibilities properly.

### 4. Outdated technology and machinery

Old equipment may operate slowly, consume more energy and experience frequent breakdowns.

Solution: Adopt modern technology, automate repetitive tasks and maintain equipment regularly.

### 5. Poor working conditions

Unsafe workplaces, inadequate lighting, excessive noise and uncomfortable working conditions can reduce employee efficiency.

Solution: Provide safe equipment, suitable facilities and a healthy working environment.

### 6. Absenteeism and employee turnover

Frequent absence and employee resignations interrupt operations and increase recruitment and training costs.

Solution: Improve employee satisfaction, provide support and maintain reasonable workloads.

### 7. Poor communication

Misunderstandings between managers and employees can cause mistakes, delays and duplication of work.

Solution: Establish clear instructions, regular meetings and effective feedback systems.

### 8. Shortage of raw materials

Insufficient materials can stop production and delay delivery.

Solution: Maintain suitable inventory levels, forecast demand and coordinate with suppliers.

### 9. Poor quality control

Defective products require rework or replacement, increasing costs and reducing productivity.

Solution: Establish quality standards, inspect materials and monitor production processes.

### 10. Lack of coordination

Departments may work independently without sharing information, causing delays and conflicting priorities.

Solution: Improve interdepartmental communication and coordinate plans and responsibilities.

### 11. Waste of resources

Unnecessary movement, excess inventory, defective materials and inefficient processes increase operating costs.

Solution: Identify waste, improve workflow and use resources carefully.

### 12. Resistance to change

Employees may resist new technology, procedures or organizational changes because of fear or lack of understanding.

Solution: Explain the benefits of change, provide training and involve employees in implementation.

## 3. Measures for improving productivity

### 1. Scientific management

Study work methods, establish efficient procedures and identify the best way to perform tasks.

### 2. Employee training and development

Improve employee knowledge, technical skills and ability to handle modern equipment.

### 3. Motivation and incentives

Use financial and non-financial rewards to encourage employees to achieve performance targets.

### 4. Improved work design

Use job rotation, job enlargement and job enrichment to improve skill utilization and reduce repetitive work.

### 5. Technology and automation

Adopt suitable machines, software and automated systems to reduce manual effort and processing time.

### 6. Effective planning and control

Set measurable targets, compare actual output with planned output and correct deviations promptly.

### 7. Quality management

Establish quality standards and monitor work processes to reduce defects, rework and customer complaints.

### 8. Preventive maintenance

Inspect and service machinery regularly to prevent breakdowns and production interruptions.

### 9. Better communication and teamwork

Encourage cooperation among departments and provide clear information about goals, duties and deadlines.

### 10. Proper resource allocation

Ensure that employees, materials, equipment and finances are available where and when required.

### 11. Performance measurement

Measure output per employee, production costs, defect rates and time taken to complete work. Use these measurements to identify areas for improvement.

### 12. Continuous improvement

Regularly review work processes, collect employee suggestions and implement improvements to reduce waste and increase efficiency.

## 4. Example

A manufacturing company experiences low productivity because of machine breakdowns and insufficient employee training.

Management introduces preventive maintenance, trains employees to operate the machines and establishes daily production targets. It also monitors defects and rewards teams that meet quality standards.

These measures reduce downtime, improve product quality and increase output.

## 5. Conclusion

Productivity problems arise from poor planning, inadequate skills, low motivation, outdated equipment, communication failures and resource wastage. Effective training, motivation, technology, quality control and continuous improvement help management increase productivity, reduce costs and improve organizational performance.

# 29(b) Explain the relationship between control and performance and discuss methods of improving performance through control. (15 Marks)

## 1. Introduction

Control is the managerial process of ensuring that actual activities conform to planned activities.

Performance refers to the results achieved by individuals, departments or the entire organization.

Control and performance are closely related because control helps managers identify performance gaps and take corrective action to achieve organizational objectives.

## 2. Relationship between control and performance

### 1. Establishes performance standards

Control begins with setting standards for expected performance.

Example: A company establishes a monthly production target of 5,000 units.

### 2. Measures actual performance

Managers collect information about actual results to determine whether employees and departments meet their targets.

Example: Actual monthly production is recorded as 4,500 units.

### 3. Compares actual and planned performance

Actual results are compared with established standards to identify deviations.

Example: The company identifies a shortfall of 500 units.

### 4. Identifies performance gaps

Control helps identify the reasons for poor performance, such as inadequate training, machine failure or material shortages.

### 5. Supports corrective action

Managers take appropriate action to eliminate the causes of deviations.

Example: The company repairs the machinery and provides additional employee training.

### 6. Improves productivity

Monitoring and correcting inefficient activities helps employees use resources more effectively.

### 7. Maintains quality standards

Control ensures that products and services meet required quality standards and customer expectations.

### 8. Encourages accountability

When responsibilities and performance standards are clearly defined, employees understand what they are expected to achieve.

### 9. Supports better decision-making

Reliable performance information helps managers decide whether to change work methods, allocate resources or revise plans.

### 10. Promotes continuous improvement

Regular monitoring helps management learn from mistakes and improve future performance.

## 3. Methods of improving performance through control

### 1. Setting clear standards

Standards should be specific, measurable, realistic and related to organizational objectives.

Examples include production targets, quality levels, sales targets and deadlines.

### 2. Performance measurement

Measure actual performance using suitable indicators.

Examples:

- Units produced per hour.
- Sales revenue.
- Production cost per unit.
- Number of defective products.
- Percentage of tasks completed on time.

### 3. Management by exception

Managers should give special attention to significant deviations from established standards rather than spending equal time on every minor variation.

This helps managers use their time efficiently.

### 4. Regular performance reporting

Prepare reports showing targets, actual results, deviations and reasons for differences.

Reports allow managers to identify problems and respond promptly.

### 5. Employee performance appraisal

Evaluate employees according to agreed responsibilities, performance standards, skills and achievements.

Use the results to identify training needs and development opportunities.

### 6. Feedback and counselling

Provide timely, constructive feedback to employees and explain how their performance can improve.

### 7. Training and development

When poor performance results from a lack of skills or knowledge, provide appropriate training and supervision.

### 8. Corrective action

Identify the root cause of a problem and take suitable corrective action.

Examples include repairing equipment, changing procedures, improving schedules and reallocating resources.

### 9. Quality control

Inspect products and monitor processes to detect defects and ensure that quality standards are maintained.

### 10. Budgetary control

Compare actual expenditure and revenue with budgeted amounts to identify overspending, low sales or inefficient resource use.

### 11. Preventive control

Anticipate possible problems and take action before they affect performance.

Examples include preventive maintenance, employee training and checking material quality before production.

### 12. Use of computers and information systems

Management information systems can collect, process and present performance data quickly.

Managers can use dashboards and reports to monitor costs, productivity, quality and deadlines.

## 4. Control process

1\. Establish standards

2\. Measure actual performance

3\. Compare results with standards

4\. Identify deviations and causes

5\. Take corrective action

## 5. Example

A sales department has a monthly target of ₹10 lakh but achieves only ₹8 lakh.

The manager compares the results with the target, reviews customer feedback, identifies inadequate sales follow-up and provides additional training. The manager then monitors sales performance in the following month.

This illustrates how control identifies performance gaps and supports improvement.

## 6. Conclusion

Control and performance are interdependent. Control measures actual results, identifies deviations and enables corrective action. Through clear standards, performance appraisal, feedback, training, reporting and preventive measures, management can improve productivity, quality and organizational effectiveness.

# 30(a) Explain the use of computers and IT in management control and discuss their role in timely decision-making. (15 Marks)

## 1. Introduction

Computers and Information Technology (IT) play an important role in management control by collecting, storing, processing and analysing organizational data.

A Management Information System (MIS) is a system that obtains, stores and analyses data to provide useful information for planning, controlling and decision-making.

Computers enable managers to monitor performance, identify deviations and make decisions quickly and accurately.

## 2. Uses of computers and IT in management control

### 1. Data collection

Computers collect information about sales, production, employee attendance, inventory and financial transactions.

Example: A retail company records daily sales through a computerized billing system.

### 2. Data storage and retrieval

Computers store large volumes of information and allow managers to retrieve it whenever required.

Example: An HR department accesses employee attendance and salary records using its information system.

### 3. Data processing and analysis

Computers process raw data and convert it into meaningful information using calculations, comparisons and statistical analysis.

Example: A company analyses monthly sales data to identify its best-performing products.

### 4. Performance monitoring

Computers help managers compare actual performance with established standards.

Example: A production dashboard shows whether the factory is meeting its daily production target.

### 5. Budgetary control

Accounting software compares actual income and expenses with budgeted amounts and identifies financial deviations.

Example: A finance manager receives an alert when departmental expenditure exceeds the approved budget.

### 6. Inventory control

Inventory management systems track stock levels, material usage and reorder requirements.

Example: A supermarket system identifies products that are running out of stock.

### 7. Quality control

Computerized systems collect quality data, identify defects and monitor whether products meet required specifications.

Example: Sensors on a production line detect products that do not meet the required dimensions.

### 8. Employee performance control

IT systems record attendance, task completion, productivity and other relevant performance indicators.

Example: A project management system shows completed and pending tasks for each team member.

### 9. Financial control

Computers support accounting, cash-flow monitoring, cost analysis and financial reporting.

Example: Accounting software identifies unusual expenditure and helps management investigate it.

### 10. Communication and coordination

Email, video conferencing, shared documents and collaboration platforms help departments exchange information quickly.

Example: The production and purchasing departments share updated material requirements through a common system.

## 3. Role of computers and IT in timely decision-making

### 1. Provides real-time information

Modern information systems can provide current information about sales, stock, production and operations.

This helps managers respond quickly to changing conditions.

### 2. Reduces decision-making time

Automated data processing and report generation reduce the time required to collect and analyse information.

### 3. Improves accuracy

Computerized calculations reduce many manual calculation errors and support more reliable decisions.

However, accuracy still depends on the quality of the data and the system.

### 4. Identifies deviations quickly

Computerized alerts can notify managers when actual performance differs significantly from planned standards.

Example: A system alerts a manager when production falls below the daily target.

### 5. Supports forecasting

Computers analyse historical information to estimate future sales, demand, costs and resource requirements.

### 6. Helps evaluate alternatives

Decision-support tools allow managers to compare alternative plans, costs and expected outcomes.

### 7. Improves resource allocation

Information systems help managers allocate employees, materials, machinery and finances according to organizational needs.

### 8. Supports coordination

Shared information enables departments to work with consistent and updated data.

### 9. Improves reporting

Computers generate reports, charts, tables and dashboards that make important information easier to understand.

### 10. Supports strategic planning

Management can analyse market conditions, customer trends, costs and operational performance to make long-term decisions.

## 4. Management Information System (MIS)

### Components of MIS

1. Hardware: Computers, printers, scanners and communication equipment.
2. Software: Programs used to process and analyse data.
3. Data: Information about employees, sales, production and finance.
4. People: Managers, analysts, programmers and system operators.
5. Communication networks: Systems that allow information to be shared between departments.

### Implementation of MIS

1\. Input data

2\. Store and retrieve information

3\. Analyse data

4\. Generate reports and outputs

5\. Make decisions

6\. Take action and monitor results

## 5. Example

A manufacturing company uses an MIS to monitor production, stock and quality.

When the system detects a shortage of raw materials, it immediately informs the purchasing manager. The manager can arrange new supplies before production stops.

Thus, IT improves the speed of information flow and supports timely decisions.

## 6. Limitations of computers and IT

1. High installation and maintenance costs.
2. Risk of cyberattacks and data loss.
3. Dependence on accurate and updated data.
4. Need for employee training.
5. Technical failures may interrupt operations.
6. Human judgement remains necessary for complex decisions.

## 7. Conclusion

Computers and IT are essential tools for modern management control. MIS, computerized reports, inventory systems and performance dashboards help managers monitor operations, identify problems and make timely decisions. Their effectiveness depends on reliable data, appropriate technology and sound managerial judgement.&#x20;

# 30(b) Explain direct and preventive control and compare their applications in management. (15 Marks)

## 1. Introduction

Control is the process of ensuring that actual performance follows planned activities and organizational standards.

Direct control and preventive control are two approaches used by managers to maintain performance, identify problems and achieve organizational objectives.

- Direct control: Identifies poor performance and takes corrective action to improve it.
- Preventive control: Attempts to eliminate the causes of poor performance before problems occur.

## 2. Direct control

Direct control involves identifying deviations in actual performance and correcting them to achieve organizational goals.

It focuses on finding problems and taking action to improve performance.

### Factors influencing direct control

1\. Uncertainty

Unexpected changes in demand, technology or operating conditions may affect performance.

2\. Lack of knowledge and experience

Employees or managers may make mistakes because they do not possess sufficient skills or experience.

3\. Lack of communication

Unclear instructions and delayed information can lead to poor performance.

4\. Lack of coordination

Poor coordination between departments may result in delays and wastage.

### Effective steps in direct control

1. Establish clear performance standards.
2. Measure actual performance.
3. Compare actual results with standards.
4. Identify deviations and their causes.
5. Take corrective action.
6. Monitor the results of the corrective action.

### Advantages of direct control

- Identifies poor performance.
- Helps correct mistakes.
- Improves accountability.
- Supports coordination.
- Helps managers achieve established targets.
- Provides information for future improvements.

### Limitations of direct control

- Problems may already have caused losses before detection.
- Corrective action may require additional time and money.
- Repeated problems may occur if their root causes are not removed.
- Excessive monitoring may reduce employee independence.

### Example

A factory discovers that its production output is below the target. The manager examines the production process, identifies a machine problem, arranges repairs and monitors output to ensure improvement.

This is direct control because corrective action is taken after poor performance is identified.

## 3. Preventive control

Preventive control aims to eliminate the causes of undesirable activities and poor management before they lead to performance problems.

It emphasizes preparation, proper procedures, evaluation and early action.

### Effective steps in preventive control

1\. Appoint qualified managers

Managers should possess the knowledge, experience and skills needed to plan and supervise activities effectively.

2\. Apply management principles

Clear planning, proper delegation, coordination and suitable procedures reduce the possibility of mistakes.

3\. Evaluate performance and processes

Regular evaluation helps identify potential weaknesses before they cause serious problems.

4\. Provide training

Employees should be trained to perform their duties correctly and use equipment safely.

5\. Establish standards and procedures

Clear rules, quality standards and operating procedures help prevent errors.

6\. Conduct regular inspections

Inspect machinery, materials and work processes to identify potential failures.

7\. Plan for risks

Identify possible problems and prepare suitable preventive measures.

### Advantages of preventive control

- Prevents problems before they occur.
- Reduces wastage and operating costs.
- Improves accuracy and quality.
- Reduces the need for corrective action.
- Supports smooth operations.
- Saves time by avoiding disruptions.

### Limitations of preventive control

- Requires planning and initial investment.
- Cannot prevent every unexpected problem.
- Depends on accurate information and proper risk assessment.
- Excessive procedures may slow down operations.

### Example

A factory regularly inspects machinery, trains operators and replaces worn components before they fail. This reduces the risk of unexpected breakdowns and production interruptions.

This is preventive control because action is taken before the problem occurs.

## 4. Comparison between direct and preventive control

| Basis          | Direct control                               | Preventive control                         |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Meaning        | Corrects identified poor performance         | Prevents potential problems                |
| Main focus     | Detection and correction                     | Prevention and preparation                 |
| Timing         | Usually after a deviation is detected        | Before a problem occurs                    |
| Approach       | Reactive                                     | Proactive                                  |
| Main objective | Restore performance to the required standard | Maintain performance by avoiding failures  |
| Cost           | May involve repair and correction costs      | May require advance investment             |
| Benefits       | Corrects existing problems                   | Reduces the chance of future problems      |
| Example        | Repairing a machine after a breakdown        | Maintaining a machine to prevent breakdown |

## 5. Applications in management

### Applications of direct control

1. Correcting production defects.
2. Addressing employee performance gaps.
3. Investigating overspending.
4. Correcting missed deadlines.
5. Resolving customer complaints.
6. Improving performance after comparing results with standards.

### Applications of preventive control

1. Preventive maintenance of machinery.
2. Training employees before assigning tasks.
3. Checking raw materials before production.
4. Establishing safety procedures.
5. Conducting risk assessments.
6. Verifying financial transactions to reduce errors and fraud.
7. Establishing quality standards before manufacturing.

## 6. Example comparing both approaches

Consider a manufacturing company that experiences repeated machine failures.

- Direct control: When a machine fails, the maintenance team repairs it and investigates the cause.
- Preventive control: The company schedules regular inspections, replaces worn components and trains operators to use the machine correctly.

Both approaches are useful, but preventive control reduces the likelihood of repeated failures, while direct control helps restore operations when problems arise.

## 7. Conclusion

Direct control focuses on identifying and correcting poor performance, whereas preventive control focuses on eliminating potential causes of problems. Preventive control helps reduce wastage and avoid failures, while direct control is essential for correcting existing deviations. An effective organization uses both approaches to maintain performance and achieve its objectives.&#x20;
